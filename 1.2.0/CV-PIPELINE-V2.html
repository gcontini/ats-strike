    CV pipeline v2: design¶

    Status: draft, 2026-11-12. The probe of section 11 has run: go, on the conditions listed there. Nothing of the build exists yet. This replaces the draft of 2026-11-10, which compiled the profile into facts and still wrote every CV with a model; section 12 says what was kept from it. When v2 ships, it replaces doc/cv-pipeline.md, which describes v1.

    Summary¶

    v1 writes every CV from scratch: about 24 model calls and five minutes to rewrite duties that are, in most CVs, the same duties. The rewriting is where the errors come from (exaggeration, lost context, garbled sentences), and the keywords a job description asks for end up crammed into the summary.

    v2 moves the writing out of the per-job path. Once per profile, the profile is compiled into a store: a tree of competences named in the market's words, and a pool of pre-written, reviewed bullets, each tagged with what it proves and how many lines it takes. For each job description, one model call places the job's requirements on the tree, and code selects the CV: which stories, at what length, in which experiences. A model then chooses between stored wordings, writes the headline and one summary sentence, and edits the job's own words in where the facts support them.

    The balance between what a keyword search wants and what a reader wants is a set of weights sent with each request, so it can change without a redeploy.

    A probe on one real profile and ten held-out job descriptions showed that the compiled pool can be trusted (4% of bullets wrong at the candidate's first reading), that the allocation is consistent (two runs share 83% of the placed importance), that the page budget holds in code (16 of 16 CVs fit at the first render), and that selection alone does not carry the job's words into the CV: literal coverage was 35% against v1's 62%, almost all of it the summary. So the varnish step's edits are part of the design, not a polish.

    1. Why: what v1 does and what it costs¶

    Measured on 51 CV runs of real applications, their 1,076 rendered bullets, and 2,547 unique job descriptions, all kept in a private data folder; then by the probe. Appendix A says how each number was counted.

    Question Measured What it means
    Does v1 tailor the selection? Of 32 profile duties, 19 are in at least 70% of CVs, 7 are never shown, and 6 really vary. 47 of 51 CVs show the same 3 experiences. The scoring of every duty against every job buys little selection.
    Where do the job's keywords end up? Requirements with one of their keywords present, weighted by importance: 40% in the profile's own text, 58% in the CV, 40% in the CV without its summary. All of v1's keyword gain is the summary: 743 characters holding 7 of the job's keywords. The rewritten bullets add none.
    Can lines be budgeted in code? lines = ceil(characters / 98) is exact for 98.8% of bullets. In the probe, a line model in points put 16 of 16 CVs on two pages at the first render. Yes. The render is a check, not a loop.
    Are bullet lengths varied? 94% of bullets are exactly 2 lines, at a median 156 of 196 characters. No variety, and about a fifth of the bullet area is blank.
    What does a CV cost? v1: a median 24 calls, 290 seconds and 221,000 tokens, 63% of them thinking. v2 in the probe: 5 to 6 calls per CV, about 30 once per profile.
    Is a job's vocabulary predictable? 1,796 distinct keywords in 51 jobs; 77% occur in one job only. A job's wording cannot be written into the bullets in advance.
    How much of a job can the profile not prove? v1: about 25% of a job's importance has no duty that proves it. v2: 18% of importance lands on ghost leaves, 0.3% nowhere. This is the ceiling of any keyword score, and it has to be reported.
    Is relevance literal? Of v1's 1,098 strong matches between a duty and a requirement, 17% have one of the requirement's keywords in the duty. Matching is mostly a judgment of meaning. Keyword matching alone cannot select.
    Can a one-time compile be trusted? 4.0% of 176 pool bullets wrong at the candidate's first reading; the review edited 12% of lines. Yes. Bullets need no validation per job.
    Does selection carry the job's words? Literal coverage 35% against v1's 62%; summary alone 19% against 53%. No. The varnish must put them in.

    2. Goals and non-goals¶

    Goals:

    1. Write once, select many times. The text of a CV is written and reviewed once per profile, not once per job.
    2. A tunable balance. Keyword coverage against context, stories and a complete career, as weights in the client's configuration.
    3. No silent gaps. An experience is left out only when the balance says so, and the earliest and the most recent are always shown.
    4. A readable summary. The job's keywords move to where they cost a reader least.
    5. Fewer moving parts per job: 5 to 6 calls instead of 24.

    Non-goals:

    • The LaTeX render, the letter, JD detection and analysis, the sessions and the event stream stay as they are.
    • No rules on metric density, bullet formulas or action verbs.
    • No external skills taxonomy. The tree is built from the profile and from what real job descriptions ask.
    • Nothing personal is stored on the server.

    3. The design at a glance¶

    flowchart TD
    PROF(["candidate_profile.json"]) -->|"once per profile version"| ITEMS["<b>items</b><br/>base items and stories"]
    JDS(["a sample of job descriptions"]) --> TREE
    ITEMS --> TREE["<b>tree</b><br/>competences, market names, aliases"]
    TREE --> POOL["<b>pool</b><br/>bullets on a ladder, groups,<br/>skill phrases, summary frames"]
    POOL --> REV["<b>review</b><br/>code checks, a pool review, the candidate"]
    REV --> STORE(["the store<br/>next to the profile, editable"])
    STORE --> START(["POST /v1/cv<br/>JD + profile + store + mix"])
    START --> ALLOC["<b>allocate</b><br/>the job's requirements onto the tree"]
    ALLOC --> SEL["select<br/>(code) content and length"]
    SEL --> FIT{"over the page limit?"}
    FIT -->|"yes"| DOWN["step a story down a rung,<br/>then remove"] --> FIT
    FIT -->|"no"| VAR["<b>varnish</b><br/>wording, the job's words,<br/>headline"]
    VAR --> HL["highlight<br/>(code)"]
    HL --> FIN(["result"])

    Terms used below:

    • base item: one statement from the profile, typed context, action or result.
    • story: the base items that tell one piece of work together.
    • node: one competence in the tree. A leaf is something a recruiter searches for. A ghost leaf is one the profile cannot prove.
    • rung: one length of a story's bullet: 1, 2 or 3 lines.
    • wording: one of the stored versions of a rung.
    • group: bullets that go into the CV together or not at all.
    • slot: one place in a planned CV, to be filled by a wording.
    • mix: the weights that balance the keyword score against the human score.

    4. The store: once per profile¶

    The store is compiled from the profile, kept next to it on the client, and editable by hand. It is personal data: it travels with each CV request, as the profile does, and the server keeps none of it.

    For the probe's profile (five experiences) the store came to 120 items in 27 stories, a tree of 176 nodes, and a pool of 162 bullets, 5 compact lines, 5 role summaries, 39 skill phrases and 5 summary frames. Compiling it took about 30 model calls.

    4.1 Base items and stories¶

    Each experience's duties are cut into numbered base items. From the fictional candidate in examples/candidate:

    {"id": "e1.i21", "experience": 1, "story": "e1.s6", "type": "action",
 "text": "Defined and documented the backup and restore procedures and the disaster recovery plan.",
 "source": ["Defined and documented the backup and restore procedures and the disaster recovery plan"],
 "agency": "did", "tags": ["disaster recovery", "operational procedures"]}
    • source is copied verbatim from the profile. Code checks that each span is a substring of its experience and drops an item that fails. In the probe the spans covered 93–97% of each experience's duty text and no item was dropped.
    • type drives repetition. Two bullets never share an action or a result; a context may recur, spelled out once.
    • agency is led, did or supported, with what the profile's notes say about scope. "I guided my team, NOT the rehearsals" stays attached to the rehearsal item.
    • References between duties ("the cloud projects above") are resolved, so each item stands alone.
    • Context belongs to the experience, not to a story. Job-level context (the system, the client, the team) is kept at the experience level and handed to every pool call. The probe first put it in stories of its own, and the bullets of a call that was not handed it came out bare.
    • A story is one system and one phase of work, not one duty of the profile. The candidate found the probe's stories "a bit granular" and merged five into two; the profile's duty split is not the unit.

    4.2 The competence tree¶

    One tree, three levels, numbered: disciplines, competences, and leaves. A job's requirements are later placed on this same tree, so a general requirement lands on a competence and a specific one on a leaf.

    It is built in this order:

    1. Collect what the market asks. Run the existing jd_evaluation on a sample of job descriptions, spread over role families and languages.
    2. Tag what the profile proves. Each base item gets competence tags.
    3. Join them. A model drafts the tree from both lists. Each node takes the market's most frequent name and lists its aliases ("Kubernetes", "K8s"). A broad node asked in many phrasings, such as stakeholder management, is split along the distinctions jobs make. A leaf nobody asks for sits under a parent that is asked for.
    4. Attach the items. A second call gives each action or result item the leaves it proves, at three levels: touches, a substantial part, is the work. A context item attaches at the first level at most: a leaf that only the situation supports ("the team worked in Agile") is weakly proven, enough for the varnish to use the word in a bullet carrying that context, never enough for a skill phrase.
    5. Add ghost leaves: the most-asked things the profile cannot prove. A requirement can then land precisely as a gap.
    6. The candidate reviews it. Numbering is then frozen; new nodes are appended.
    7. Test it on jobs it was not built from: how much importance lands on a proven node, on a ghost, or nowhere.

    What the probe taught about the tree:

    • proven must rest on more than one weak attachment. Two attach calls on the same tree agreed on every level-3 pair, but six leaves flipped between proven and ghost because each hung on a single level-1 or level-2 item. A leaf is proven by two calls, or the candidate checks every leaf with one weak item.
    • Some ghosts are attachment misses. Two operating-system leaves took importance as ghosts while a story is mixed-OS administration. The held-out test lists the ghosts an item plausibly proves.
    • Aliases need their own check. About 20 nodes came with aliases that are a narrower thing, a broader thing or another product, against an explicit rule. Each node keeps two lists: what is the same thing, and what counts as it. Only the first may ever replace printed words.
    • Compound names hide the plain term. A node called "DevOps & CI/CD" never occurs literally in a job or a bullet; the plain term is the name or an alias.
    • Fixed sections prove too. Languages, certifications and the skills list are not items; a leaf they prove carries an outside note and counts.
    • Placement is loosest where the tree is coarse: the job furthest from the profile's centre had the lowest agreement between two allocations (66%). The corpus sample is where the margins get detail.

    The tree's names and aliases are also what gets printed in the skills block and in the summary's keyword slot. That is why the naming matters.

    4.3 The pool¶

    • Bullets on a ladder. Each story is written at 1, 2 and 3 lines, to the line grid, and each rung in 2 or 3 wordings. For the disaster recovery story of the example candidate:
    Rung Text
    1 line Wrote the disaster recovery plan of the Games' results system against a 2-hour RTO.
    2 lines Wrote the backup, restore and disaster recovery procedures of the Games' results system, a core system with a 2-hour RTO. My team tested them at the full-scale rehearsals and met the SLA.
    • Groups. A longer story told over two or three bullets, or bullets that refer to each other, form a group. It is selected, placed and trimmed as one unit, so no bullet ever refers to one that was cut. Up to 7 lines; a story that joins several pieces of work, up to 9. The candidate's verdict on the probe: "I do love the groups".
    • The role summary comes first. It is written before the ladder and handed to the ladder call, so a bullet carries only the context the header and the role summary do not. Five of the candidate's thirteen notes on the probe's pool were bullets repeating what the header or the role summary already said.
    • Compact lines. One line per experience, dense with terms, for a job shown only to keep the career whole.
    • Skill phrases, composed where it helps: "Terraform IaC", "CI/CD with Jenkins". A phrase needs a leaf proven at level 3, or by two items: the two wrong phrases of the probe each rested on one weak attachment.
    • Summary frames, one per role family: pre-written text with <<job_title>>, a slot for a few keywords, and a slot for one <<sentence>>. They were the weakest pieces of the probe's pool, written far from the items: their numbers and years are checked in code against the profile, and they are reviewed at compile time.

    Each bullet and group carries: the items it uses and those it fully covers, its nodes and how fully it proves each, its line count, the terms it contains, and two human factors scored once, from 0 to 2: context and success.

    4.4 Review¶

    Code checks first: length against the rung, every number present in the items, "led" or "managed" only where an item's agency says so, no bullet made of context alone, group size. Then a model reviews each experience's pool against the profile, with minimal edits. Then the candidate reads the pool, marking each piece ok, taste or wrong, with a note; the marked file is the store's editing loop, and a marked bullet is rewritten from the note.

    Two rules the probe forced:

    • The pool review has its own prompt. v1's review, shown one story's ladder alone, read the wordings and rungs as repetitions and replaced 7 of 23 bullets with other stories' facts. Shown the whole experience, told that a story's bullets are alternatives of which one is printed, and allowed to edit only the new pieces, it behaved.
    • An edit that breaks the line limit is refused. One review edit took a three-line bullet to four.

    The review edited 12% of the probe's lines, mostly exaggerations ("led" for a part of the work, "eliminated" for "saved"). It is not consistent across wordings and missed exaggerations the candidate caught, so the candidate's reading stays the last check. It is sized for a quarter of an hour at a time.

    The catalog the evaluation's judge uses stays out of this step: a generator tuned on its grader cannot be graded by it.

    Every bullet is keyed to its source items, so a profile change regenerates only what it touches, and a hand-edited bullet is locked.

    5. Per job description¶

    5.1 Allocate¶

    One model call, about a minute. It extracts the job's requirements as jd_evaluation does today, each with its importance, and places each on the tree:

    {"name": "Container orchestration in production", "importance": 80,
 "terms": ["Kubernetes"], "node": "3.2.1", "relation": "same"}
    Relation Meaning Points on the node
    same, broader the node is what is asked, or the job asks more generally importance
    narrower the job asks something more specific than the node half
    sibling the node is a ghost leaf (code labels this; the model is not told which leaves are ghosts) importance, on the ghost
    none nothing in the tree none; the requirement is reported as unmatched

    Points flow up at half per level, and what a node inherits is capped at its largest single contribution, so a discipline and its own skills do not count twice.

    The job's literal terms are the requirement's keywords in English, as jd_evaluation returns them, plus the job's own words when the text has them. The probe counted only words found in the job text, which left the selector blind to every requirement of a job written in Italian (six of the ten) while the measurement counted them. A same term the tree did not know comes back as a pending alias for the store; the probe produced 47 in ten jobs, too many to apply unreviewed.

    5.2 Select¶

    Pure code, about two seconds. It decides what is said and how many lines it takes, for the whole CV at once:

    • which stories, at which rung, or as a group;
    • which experiences are shown in full, in compact form, or not at all. The earliest and the most recent are always shown;
    • the 10 skill phrases, on 2 lines, taken by the job's terms they add;
    • the summary frame and the terms for its keyword slot.

    It starts from the two mandatory experiences in compact form and repeatedly takes the move with the best gain per line under the objective of section 6: add a story, step one up a rung, open an experience with its two best slots at any rung, add one in compact form. It goes on with the least-loss move while one fits, so the page is filled; then a refill pass drops the weakest slot and runs again, keeping the result if it scores higher. Inside a job, bullets go most relevant first, except that a story introducing the job's system comes before stories that assume it.

    Each of the first three rules is a correction from the probe: a stop-at-no-gain rule left one CV at 31 of 71 lines; opening an experience with two one-line bullets kept a core experience compact in 6 of 10 CVs; and the greedy is path-dependent, so one job scored lower on the keyword score at a higher keyword weight.

    5.3 Render and fit¶

    The plan is rendered and its pages counted. While it runs over the limit, a story steps down a rung, or a group becomes its single bullet; only when no story can step down is something removed. The line model is in points, calibrated from two renders and one check of the template: the line pitch, the gap between items (a third of a line each), the header of a full and of a compact experience. In the probe it put 16 of 16 CVs on two pages at the first render, with one to four lines to spare.

    5.4 Varnish¶

    A model reads the assembled CV as a whole and:

    • puts the job's words in, in the summary first and then in the bullets, through the existing replace_in_* tools. Code refuses an edit that adds a number, a technology or a line, and the existing validation prompt checks the result. This is the step the probe made mandatory: selection alone reached 35% literal coverage against v1's 62%, and the body review rightly strips terms the profile does not back;
    • picks one stored wording per slot, for varied openers, rhythm and the job's words;
    • flags two slots that tell the same fact; code replaces one with the selector's next candidate. Shared context is not a repeat: that was every flag the probe raised;
    • regenerates a bullet it cannot use, in three tiers: a tool returns the story's other stored bullets with the same line count; failing that, a small local edit (a reference the role summary already explains, "the" for a thing not yet introduced), saved and applied at once, checked for its line count; failing that, a new sentence from a model that sees the profile, the pool, the story, the requested edit and the scores, which waits for the candidate's approval before it joins the pool. The last tier comes last in the build.

    A separate call writes the headline and the summary's one sentence. The headline is the job's title when the profile's titles license it; otherwise it is written from the job, the experiences shown and the allowed titles. In the probe every headline stayed within the titles held or allowed.

    The model chooses between reviewed sentences, so it cannot exaggerate or garble. Code checks each reply and falls back to a default wording, so a failed call still yields a CV.

    5.5 Highlight¶

    In code, from spans marked in the pool: the job's terms in bold, heaviest first, a few per CV, taken from the allocation's English keywords (the job's own words gave an Italian job none).

    6. Scoring and the mix¶

    The keyword score is measured against everything the job asks, unmatched requirements included. It has two parts:

    • literal: a first occurrence of any of a requirement's terms counts its weight in full, a second occurrence a third more;
    • proof: per node, the best piece counts in full, the next a third, the next a fifth, capped at one. A compact line is a piece.

    The divisors 1, 3, 5 make covering a new requirement worth more than repeating one, and give each requirement a share of the CV close to its share of the job [2]. A sum of this shape is monotone submodular, the class for which greedy selection under a budget is the standard method [1].

    The human score has five terms:

    Term Counted from
    continuity each experience left out costs its share of the career's years
    completeness the action and result items the CV covers, over the whole career
    rhythm the mix of 1-, 2- and 3-line bullets
    context the pre-scored context of the chosen bullets
    success their pre-scored success

    The first three are counted in code on the selection. The last two are judged once per bullet when the pool is compiled, where the candidate can read and correct them. Nothing is judged per job: a model scoring on a fine scale is inconsistent from one call to the next [3], and v1's scores were multiples of 5 in 99.9% of cases.

    The mix is a table in the client's cv.toml, sent with each request as pages already is:

    [mix]
ats = 0.5           # 0 = human only, 1 = keyword score only
literal = 0.5       # inside the keyword score: the job's own words against proven coverage
continuity = 1.0    # the human terms, relative to each other
completeness = 1.0
context = 1.0
success = 1.0
rhythm = 0.5

    In the probe the human score stayed within 0.04 across every plan and every mix, so the lever moved the CV little: context and success as means penalize every added bullet. The weights stay internal until the evaluation shows they matter, and the two pre-scored terms are to be reworked as page-normalized sums.

    7. What gets deleted¶

    • profile_scoring and the |3-80,7-40 score suffixes.
    • format_selection and the formats.
    • The per-experience writers and their validation rounds.
    • The highlight call.
    • capped_coverage.
    • The two meta requirements added to every job, "success stories" and "concrete examples": they become the pool's success and context scores.

    8. How v2 keeps the house rules¶

    • The pipeline is a library. Points, selection, fit and highlight are pure functions of values. The store arrives with the request.
    • No hidden global state. The mix and the page geometry are arguments.
    • Contracts. The store gets a pydantic model in cv_contracts.
    • Tests. The pure functions get unit tests on hand-made values; the graph test keeps the fake HTTP client inside a real ModelSelector; the golden render stays.

    9. Evaluation¶

    • Thorough runs decide, v1 against v2, on the same day with the same judge. Literal coverage is scored on both v1's key points and v2's allocation.
    • New columns in the evaluation, checked in code on any CV: pages, the career span shown, the headline against the job's title, the summary's length, and bullets that refer to another bullet outside a group. Each is a feature that changed when v1 replaced the pipeline before it: CVs showing the whole career fell from 62% to 6%, headlines naming the job's kind of role from 66% to 36%, the summary grew from 528 to 734 characters, and bullets referring to another rose from 0.2% to 1.9%. v2 passes most of these by construction, so they guard against regressions more than they measure progress.
    • The human score has no ground truth beyond the candidate's own reading. Three checks, in this order: blind pairwise reading of CVs built at different mixes, which also sets the weights; a direction check on CVs already sent, where the score should rate the set that did better in practice above the other; and the evaluation's readability and style columns, which stay independent of the pipeline. The probe could do only three blind pairs (v2 preferred in two); the rest is the evaluation's, in short sessions.
    • Similarity between the CV and the job stays an evaluation column. In the probe, the tree's top-eight stories fell four to eight inside the stories the selector chose, an embedding ranking three to seven; nothing says which is right until the candidate's own picks are measured.

    10. Risks¶

    Risk Mitigation
    An unusual job gets generic bullets: the pool cannot reframe a story for a requirement nobody anticipated the varnish's regeneration tiers; the unmatched report shows when it happens
    The job's words stay out: selection alone reached 35% literal coverage the varnish's edits are on the critical path, and the evaluation scores coverage on both key-point sets
    The tree's abstract nodes do not discriminate, and a leaf on one weak item flips split along the distinctions jobs make; proven by two calls or the candidate's check
    A wrong alias prints a false claim two alias lists per node; only "same thing" may replace words, and a new alias waits for review
    The review misses what the candidate catches the candidate's reading is the last check, sized for a quarter of an hour
    The scores are proxies with no outcome data behind them few weights, kept internal until the evaluation shows they move the CV
    A client's own template has its own geometry the line constants are calibrated from the template; the render stays the judge

    11. Plan of work¶

    The probe ran first, with no repository change, in a git-ignored scratch folder on the private data: items, tree and pool for one real profile; ten held-out job descriptions allocated twice, selected, varnished and rendered; the candidate reviewed the items and tree, the whole pool, and three blind pairs against v1.

    Criterion Result
    G1 the candidate marks at most 5% of the pool's bullets as wrong pass: 4.0% at the first reading, 1.2% after one rewrite
    G2 two allocations share at least 80% of their points; at most 10% of importance lands nowhere pass: 83%; 0.3% nowhere
    G3 keyword coverage at least v1's, with a summary under 500 characters and 10 skills on 2 lines fail: 35% against 62%; the summary and skills limits held
    G4 two pages with the earliest and the most recent job; the first render fits in 8 of 10 pass: 16 of 16 at the first render
    G5 the candidate prefers the new CV in at least 7 of 10 blind pairs direction only: 2 of 3; ten pairs were too many to read

    Go, on four conditions: the varnish puts the job's words in (G3); the selector fills the page, opens experiences with their best slots and counts completeness over the whole career; the pool review has its own prompt; context sits at the experience level.

    Then the build, one sub-plan each:

    Sub-plan Output
    S1 Base items and stories the decomposition prompt, the source check, item types; context at the experience level; stories by system and phase
    S2 Competence tree the corpus sample, the shape and attach prompts, proven on more than one weak attachment, the alias check, ghost leaves, the held-out test
    S3 Pool role summary first, ladder, wordings, groups, compact lines, skill phrases on strong leaves, summary frames with checks, pre-scores
    S4 Review and store the pool review prompt, edits refused over the limit, the marking loop, file format, lock rules, the compile endpoint and client command
    S5 Allocation the prompt, relations, point rules, English literal terms, the unmatched report, pending aliases
    S6 Selector and scores the objective, the mix, fill and refill, bullet order, the plan report
    S7 Geometry and render line constants from calibration; compact experience and skills block in the template
    S8 Varnish the job's words in, the pick, the repeat check, regeneration tiers one and two, headline and sentence
    S9 Highlight marked spans, rules in code
    S10 Protocol, client, deletion of v1 request parts, the mix, artifacts, docs
    S11 Evaluation the new columns; a thorough run of v1 against v2; the reading checks in short sessions
    S12 Regeneration, tier three a new sentence written on demand

    The wire changes (a compile endpoint, the store and the mix as request parts, the model roles) are decided in S4 and S10. v2 is 2.0.0.

    12. Alternatives considered¶

    • Writing each CV from compiled facts (the draft of 2026-11-10). It fixed the units but kept a writer and a verifier in every CV, which is where v1's errors and cost are. Kept from it: facts and stories, the diminishing coverage objective, header costs instead of formats, highlight in code, and the fit rule.
    • Validating every bullet per job. The probe showed it is not needed: 4% of pool bullets were wrong at the candidate's first reading, and the review loop plus the candidate's marks brought it to about 1%.
    • A tree per job description, matched to the candidate's. Aligning two trees is noisier than placing each requirement on a fixed one, and jd_evaluation already overlaps with itself by only about half from run to run.
    • Similarity of bullets to the job as the selector. It knows no importance, says "similar" rather than "proves", and has no diminishing returns, so the bullets nearest the job are near-duplicates of each other. It is also what the evaluation measures.
    • Swapping aliases for the job's words in code. It never fired in the probe (0 swaps in 16 CVs), and with the aliases as the model wrote them it would have swapped one product for another. The model's guarded edits do the job.
    • A model choosing the content. format_selection chose the same format in about 96% of its decisions until its prompt was rebalanced.
    • Code choosing the wording. Code cannot judge how a page reads.
    • A long skills list as the keyword carrier. Rejected for the reader: 10 items on 2 lines.
    • A summary written whole per job, or pre-written whole. The first is what gets stuffed; the second carries none of the job's words. Frames with slots sit between them, and the varnish edits the job's words into the frame.
    • A hard rule against career gaps. An irrelevant job may be left out; it costs in proportion to its length.

    13. Open questions¶

    1. Bullet order. Most relevant first, or the story that explains the job's system first? The probe tried the former with one exception rule.
    2. Allocation. One call that extracts and places (the probe's choice), or two?
    3. Similarity. A selection term with its own weight, a check on the allocation, or evaluation only?
    4. Geometry. Constants in the client's configuration, or measured from a calibration render of the template (the probe's choice)?
    5. The human terms. Means, sums normalized by the page, or something that moves the CV when the mix moves?

    Appendix A: how the numbers were measured¶

    All from the artifacts each v1 run stores under steps/, the rendered PDFs and the job descriptions in the private data folder; the probe's numbers from its own outputs in a git-ignored folder. The scripts were throwaway; these are the counting rules.

    • Selection. A CV duty maps to the profile duty of the same experience with the same score suffix. A duty's rate is the share of runs that show it, over duties present in at least 10 runs.
    • Keywords. For each run, the requirements of key_points.json without the two meta items. A requirement counts when one of its keywords occurs as a whole word, ignoring case, in the text measured: the profile, the final CV, or the CV without its summary. Shares are weighted by importance and averaged over runs. The probe scored its CVs by the same rule, on v1's key points of the same ten jobs and on its own allocation's keywords.
    • Lines. Each bullet of the final document is matched to its lines in the PDF's text layout. The model ceil(characters / 98) is compared with the count. The probe's line model in points was calibrated from the text positions of two renders.
    • Cost. The last line of each run's log; for the probe, one line per model request.
    • Vocabulary. Keywords of all requirements, lower-cased.
    • Unproven demand. The importance of requirements whose best duty score is under 40, over the run's total; for the probe, the importance landing on ghost leaves or on no node.
    • Literal against judged matches. For each match scored 60 or more, whether any keyword of its requirement occurs in the duty.
    • Market terms. Regular expressions, in English and Italian, over the unique job descriptions.
    • Pool quality. The candidate marked every bullet ok, taste or wrong; the wrong share is over all bullets. Review edits are counted per line.
    • Allocation consistency. The two runs' point vectors, after the flow upward, normalized; the shared mass is the sum of the minimum per node.
