Goal: find what the recruiter is looking for in a candidate, as an ATS would screen for it: the responsibilities, skills and
technologies the job description (JD) asks for, each scored by importance.
Input: one JD.
Output: a JSON list of 6 to 18 items, most important first:

```json
[{"id": 1, "requirement": "Engineering manager", "keywords": ["Engineering Manager", "Engineering Lead"], "importance": 100}]
```

How: in your reasoning, split the JD into leaves, score each leaf, group the leaves into items, score each item from its leaves,
then review the whole list. Output the items, not the leaves.

- **What an item can be:**
  - Item 1: the job title itself. Its keywords are the title and its common variants.
  - A responsibility: an activity that 2 or more of the JD's responsibility bullets involve.
  - A skill: the leaves of one stack or one practice.
  - A discipline: the field that 2 or more skills belong to.
  - A leaf that fits no group.
  - English: when the JD is written in English, one requirement can be "English" (if not superseeded by other items); importance 10-30, more when the JD stresses it or mention international communication.
  - The last two items, below.
- **Leave out:**
  - Generic candidate traits (eg. "motivated", "team player" ...). Keep the skills the JD requires that a CV line can prove, such as stakeholder management or technical leadership.
  - Years of experience and seniority: they describe the whole career, not one line of a CV.
  - Degrees and education.
  - Company benefits, company facts, in general what the company offers.
- **Procedure, in your reasoning:**
  1. Leaves: each bullet or sentence of the JD's responsibilities and of its requirements, and each sentence of the description that asks for
     something concrete. Note for each leaf its section, its position and the qualifier it carries.
  2. Score each leaf:
     - Qualifier: the words the JD qualifies a requirement with set its range:
       strong ("excellent", "deep", "proven", "strong", "solid", "ottima", "approfondita", "comprovata", "solida", "spiccata"): 75-90;
       good ("good", "buona"): 60-75;
       plain ("knowledge of", "experience with", "understanding of", "conoscenza", "comprensione") or no qualifier: 40-60;
       weak ("familiarity", "a plus", "preferred", "desirable", "nice to have", "gradita", "preferenziale"): 10-30.
       "A strong plus" counts as plain.
     - Hedges: a leaf the JD offers an alternative to ("or a strong interest in", "or willingness to learn", "o predisposizione") is weak,
       even when the JD puts it first.
     - Responsibilities: the verb is the qualifier, it says how much of the activity the candidate owns:
       strong, owns the result ("lead", "own", "manage", "design", "define", "drive", "coordinate", "ensure", "be responsible for",
       "guidare", "gestire", "progettare", "definire", "coordinare", "garantire", "essere responsabile"): 70-85;
       plain, does the work ("analyze", "develop", "plan", "monitor", "verify", "evaluate", "propose",
       "analizzare", "sviluppare", "pianificare", "monitorare", "verificare", "valutare", "proporre"): 55-70;
       weak, helps someone else do it ("participate", "support", "cooperate with", "contribute to", "assist",
       "partecipare", "supportare", "collaborare", "contribuire", "affiancare"): 40-55.
       The verb levels are relative to this JD: they rank its responsibilities against each other. When most bullets use strong verbs,
       the verb does not tell them apart: spread them over 55-85 by position and insistence.
       The activities the role is about, the ones its title implies and the JD lists first, can reach 80 or more.
     - Position: within its range, what the JD puts first scores higher.
  3. Group the leaves into items:
     - Attach: a leaf joins an item that already exists when one CV line would usually prove both (e.g. "solution design", "technical design"
       and "design artifacts"), wherever in the JD the leaf comes from; otherwise it starts a new item. One item per distinct capability.
     - Skill: the leaves of one stack or one practice are one item, and the leaves are its keywords, not items of their own:
       a language with its frameworks, libraries and build tools; a cloud provider with its services; a methodology with its frameworks.
     - Discipline: the discipline the skills belong to (software engineering, software architecture, DevOps engineering, project management,
       IT service management ...) is an item too, next to its skills, when it groups 2 or more skills or 3 or more leaves.
       Check every group of skills for its discipline: a JD that asks for a language stack, a database and engineering practices
       (version control, testing) asks for software engineering too. A discipline that is the job title itself is item 1: do not repeat it.
     - Responsibility: for each activity, count the responsibility bullets that involve it. An activity found in 2 or more bullets is an item
       of its own, even when it sounds generic. For example cross-team collaboration, when bullets say "together with the functional team",
       "with the PMO", "with the other technical teams"; client and stakeholder management, when bullets say "understand the internal and
       external client's request", "agree the dates with the business".
     - The tree, for example:
       ```
       DevOps engineering                 <- discipline: groups skills
       ├─ Infrastructure automation       <- skill: groups the leaves of one stack or practice
       │   ├─ Terraform (strong)          <- leaves, each with its qualifier
       │   ├─ Ansible (plain)
       │   └─ GitLab CI (plain)
       ├─ Monitoring (plain)              <- a leaf that is a skill on its own
       └─ Linux administration (good)
       ```
     - Climb no higher than a skill a recruiter would search for and that tells CVs apart: "software engineering", "software architecture",
       "stakeholder management" are fine; "IT", "technology", "experience", "management" are too generic.
  4. Score each item from its leaves:
     - An item starts at the score of its strongest leaf.
     - Insistence: it scores more the more leaves attach to it, above all leaves from different sections or bullets
       (a responsibility found in 4 bullets scores more than one found in 2).
     - Disciplines are scored in step 5.
  5. Review the whole list, in this order:
     a. Overlap: a discipline next to its own skills shares their evidence: a CV line that proves a skill fills part of the discipline too.
        Give the discipline only what it alone carries:
        - inferred (the JD does not name it) and all its leaves are in its skill items: about 50;
        - inferred, with leaves that no skill item carries: 55-70, more the more such leaves;
        - named by the JD: the score of the leaf that names it, minus 10 when 2 or more of its skills are items.
        Never below 45: the discipline stays in the list, its name is a keyword the ATS searches for.
        An inferred discipline never scores above its best skill.
     b. Qualifiers: within the requirements, an item never scores below an item of a weaker qualifier level.
        Disciplines are exempt: their skills carry the qualifier.
     c. Spread: the few items the role is about score 80 or more; the usual requirements 40-70;
        minor ones and nice to have 10-30.
- **Last two items:**
  - requirement: "success stories", keywords: ["compliments", "success", "resulting in"], importance in the low-middle: about 40, compared with the other items.
  - requirement: "concrete examples" (specific cases, figures or results that back a skill), keywords: ["example", "results"], importance mid-low.
- **`requirement`:** a short English phrase that describe ONE skill or activity.
  - Name the SINGLE activity, not a list of categories: "Technology selection aligned with enterprise standards", not "Technologies, platforms and tools".
  - ONE means one capability, not one technology: "Java backend development" is one skill, with Spring Boot, JPA and Maven as its keywords.
- **`keywords`:**
  - One to eight literal terms an ATS would search for in an English CV: the usual English form, common variants and abbreviations ("Kubernetes", "K8s").
  - English only: translate the JD's wording ("gestione fornitori" -> "Vendor Management").
  - A keyword appears in one item only. A discipline's keywords name it ("DevOps Engineering", "DevOps"), not the terms of the skills it groups.
  - No near-duplicates: when several terms would match the same CV lines, keep one ("Application Landscape" covers "Application Landscape Assessment").
  - Take them from what the JD says. Do not add standards, tools or certifications it does not name.
  - Each keyword must tell CVs apart. If it would appear in almost any IT CV, it is too generic: experience, team, skills, IT, technologies, tools, platforms, systems, solutions, software, environments, integration. Make it specific ("Integration Architecture") or leave it out.
- **`importance`:** an integer from 0 to 100: the item's score after the review (steps 4 and 5). Item 1 is 100.
